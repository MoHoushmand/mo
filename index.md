## What you will find here

**Quantum mechanics and computation.** Entanglement, teleportation, measurement and quantum repeaters,
with short quizzes after each idea.

**Quantum computer hardware.** How spin qubits in silicon, superconducting transmons, nitrogen-vacancy
centres in diamond and topological qubits are built and operated, from the device physics to the
control electronics.

**Quantum machine learning.** A series of notebooks, from classical and quantum probability to
variational circuits, kernel methods and quantum phase estimation, with their stored results.

**Mathematics, data and software.** Computational fluid dynamics and the Navier–Stokes equations,
statistics for data science, and Zipminator, the post-quantum encryption software I build.

**Art.** Fluid-art paintings and the acrylic-pouring course I teach, in Norwegian.

Most of the site is in English; the painting course is in Norwegian, with an English summary.

## From turbulence to quantum

My way into quantum computing ran through fluid mechanics. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and
direct numerical simulation (DNS) both solve the Navier–Stokes equations; DNS resolves every scale of a
turbulent flow and is so expensive that only a handful of groups can run it at scale. Working on those
simulations, and on the high-performance materials they describe, led me to ask what a quantum computer
could do for such problems, and from there to the quantum mechanics underneath.

The hardware part of this site follows that path from the device up. I give topological qubits extra
space: anyons, the quasi-particles behind them, were predicted in Oslo by Jon Magne Leinaas and Jan
Myrheim, and I expect topological, anyon-based qubits to matter a great deal for fault-tolerant quantum
computing.

## Dedication

This site owes most to three teachers. Their stories are on the [About](about/about.md) page:
Professor **Hans Petter Langtangen** (1962–2017), who taught me numerical methods for partial
differential equations; Professor **Peter Wittek** (1980–2019), who introduced me to quantum machine
learning; and Professor **Jon Magne Leinaas**, who taught me quantum mechanics and, with Jan Myrheim,
predicted anyons.

I am also grateful to TU Delft and the University of Toronto, whose courses on edX shaped the hardware
and quantum machine learning parts of this site. Each of those parts credits its sources.
