## Background

I am an artist at heart, with a diverse background in applied mathematics, physics, data science and
entrepreneurship. My journey began in art and grew into an appreciation of the synergy between art and
science, especially between fluid art and fluid mechanics.

I hold an MSc in Applied Mathematics, Mechanics and Numerical Physics from the University of Oslo (UiO),
a BSc in Civil Engineering from Oslo and Akershus University College of Applied Sciences (HiOA, now
OsloMet), and a BA in Design and Modelling Techniques from ESMOD Bordeaux/Oslo. I have also earned a number
of professional certifications and specialisations in mathematics for machine learning, quantum machine
learning and quantum computer hardware.

## Achievements

I had the privilege of proofreading for Professor Hans Petter Langtangen, most notably his book *Finite
Difference Computing with Partial Differential Equations: A Modern Software Approach*.

In 2017 I joined as one of the first data scientists at the AI Lab funded by the Norwegian Labour and
Welfare Administration (NAV). In 2022 I rejoined NAV, and deepened my skills in data handling,
infrastructure design and full-stack development. My time at NAV was transformative: I developed tools
such as Zipminator for secure data handling, and worked on a range of data-science projects. Zipminator
has since grown into post-quantum encryption software, a QDaria product.

## Entrepreneurial ventures

Between 2017 and 2021 I worked on ventures in quantum technologies. First, in 2018, as CTO of iProud,
backed by Xplorico and Malaysian officials, we aimed to build a personal coach on hybrid machine-learning
algorithms. Then, from 2019, as CEO of QDaria, Norway's first quantum-computer company, elected by the
board, our vision was to build a quantum computer to tackle complex global problems. QDaria had four
departments: quantum computer hardware engineering, a quantum AI lab, a synthetic biology lab and a
Q-investment hub.

Our goal was to make an impact on health technology: bioinformatics, disease diagnosis, prediction and
drug discovery, using machine learning, quantum machine learning and quantum computer hardware.

## About this website

Here I share notes, code and resources in an interactive Jupyter Book, to demystify quantum subjects.
The topics range from the basics of quantum mechanics to the potential of anyons in quantum computation.
I also discuss turbulent flows and the Navier–Stokes equations.

I invite you to explore and to send me feedback. Dive into the intersection of art and science; I hope you
find both inspiration and knowledge.

## Interdisciplinary pursuits

I believe deeply that interdisciplinary collaboration unlocks the potential of emerging technologies. As an
artist, applied mathematician, physicist and data scientist, I try to bridge art and science, and this
website shows my artwork alongside my analytical projects. My work spans fluid art, fluid mechanics,
turbulent flow, data science, computational science and quantum computing.

## Fluid art and fluid mechanics

My courses on the artistic side of fluid art, currently in Norwegian, are taught at
[tegneskole.no](https://www.tegneskole.no/), and the course notes are on this site; an English version is
on its way. The scientific side, the fluid mechanics of how acrylic paints flow and interact on a canvas,
is written in English. The first is visual; the second is technical, a deep dive into applied mathematics
and mechanics, but I encourage everyone to start with the basics and work up to the equations.

## From turbulence to quantum

My curiosity lies where science meets art, and where turbulent flow meets quantum mechanics. It led me from
the macroscopic world of classical physics to the microscopic world of quantum mechanics. Here I share how
my passion for high-performance materials and for computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations of
turbulent flows brought me into the quantum domain.

The mathematical backbone is CFD and direct numerical simulation (DNS), both devoted to solving the
Navier–Stokes equations. DNS resolves every scale of a turbulent flow; it is so demanding that only a few
groups in the world have the means to run it at scale.

## Quantum exploration

Quantum computers, which may reshape our technological future, are one of my main interests. I walk you
through my quantum journey, from the basics to quantum machine learning and topological quantum computers
(TQC). I cover the mathematics underneath, from linear algebra to quantum circuits, and how it comes alive
in quantum hardware.

I have gathered many notes, code examples and resources on quantum computing here, in an interactive Jupyter
Book format, for a deep dive into quantum technologies.

A large part of this portfolio is about quantum machine learning and topological quantum computing, and the
potential of anyons in quantum computation. I believe anyon-based systems will lead the next wave of quantum
technology, and I aim to contribute to that work.

## Engage and learn

These subjects are hard to master, but I am here to guide you. With active engagement, visualisation and
patience you can grasp them, and with steady effort the concepts become clearer over time.

I value your insights and feedback. If you have suggestions or corrections for any part of this website,
please share them.

## Dedication

::::{grid} 1
:::{grid-item-card}

I am a passionate supporter of education and personal growth, and I consider myself very fortunate to have
been guided by some remarkable minds. I dedicate this space to three of them. Above all, they set an example
of kindness and respect for others, whatever their rank or status, and they guided me to the subjects that
take up most of my time and most of this site. Two of them died far too early, in 2017 and 2019.
:::
::::

### Hans Petter Langtangen (1962–2017)

First, I honour my former professor and greatest inspiration, the award-winning professor, educator and
visionary [**Hans Petter Langtangen**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Petter_Langtangen), who died of
pancreatic cancer in 2017. Between his diagnosis in early 2015 and his death in the autumn of 2017, he
wrote and finished a series of books on scientific computing and engineering, published by Springer.

He fostered an environment that encouraged you to perform beyond your own idea of your abilities. He firmly
believed that anyone, however hard their goal, could succeed with dedication and hard work. He was convinced
that there were no "stupid" people, and he could persuade even the most self-doubting of us that we could
achieve great things. He was part of the Center for Neuroplasticity, and his teaching gave me a lasting love
of numerical methods for partial differential equations.

I am proud to have proofread some of his writing, and to have contributed to his last book, *Finite
Difference Computing with Partial Differential Equations: A Modern Software Approach*. I think of him every
day, with a mixture of grief and gratitude. Rest in peace, Maestro.

### Peter Wittek (1980–2019)

Second, I honour the memory of the adventurous and inspiring professor
[**Peter Wittek**](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-s-peter-wittek-who-will-be-remembered-feb-3-event-why-future-quantum)
of the University of Toronto. He introduced me to quantum machine learning in 2017, when I had only just
begun to explore machine learning, natural language processing, topic modelling and latent Dirichlet
allocation (LDA) in earnest. At the time I was an intern working as a data scientist in what was then the
first officially funded AI lab in Norway. Although I was a novice, I found Professor Wittek's teaching
invaluable, helped by a background in physics that let me grasp these ideas with considerable effort.

His life was cut short in a Himalayan avalanche in 2019. I still believe that, had he been with us, our
third attempt to found a quantum-computing start-up would have succeeded. His death is a profound loss to
the scientific community and to everyone who knew him. I will remember him not only for his pioneering work
in quantum machine learning, but for his generosity in guiding me through what was then a completely new
field, and later for his faith in me and in our, perhaps in hindsight over-ambitious, attempt to found the
first quantum-computer company in Norway. May he rest in peace.

### Jon Magne Leinaas

I am privileged to salute my former professor in non-relativistic quantum mechanics (Modern Quantum
Mechanics), a giant of a mentor and, more importantly, a wonderful human being:
[**Jon Magne Leinaas**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Magne_Leinaas). With his friend **Jan Myrheim**,
he predicted **anyons** almost 50 years ago, in 1977. Their prediction was confirmed independently, twice,
in different laboratories during the pandemic (see, for instance, "Welcome anyons" in *Nature*). Jon is a
member of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, which awards the Nobel Prize in Physics, and many consider
him and Jan strong candidates for it. Anyons sit between fermions and bosons, and they could transform
quantum computing, with a large, positive impact on humanity.

After covering the many types of qubit, I turn my attention to topological quantum computers. I intend to
devote much of my future work to this direction, and to explain every facet of the discovery of anyons. This
new class of particles is a profound advance in our understanding of quantum mechanics. Anyons are the
theoretical basis of topological quantum computers, a computing paradigm that uses the properties of these
exotic quasi-particles for computation. They offer a promising route to fault-tolerant quantum computation,
one of the most significant challenges in the field.

I am convinced that topological quantum computers, built on anyon-based qubits, will lead the next wave of
advances in quantum computing, because fault tolerance and stability are built into them, and those are
exactly what practical, large-scale quantum computers need. Through this work I aim to show the potential of
anyon-based topological quantum computers and their potential to lead the future of quantum computing. It
pays homage to the work of Leinaas, and I hope it contributes to our shared understanding of this promising
technology. I believe that by explaining how these systems work, we can inspire and speed up further research
and development, and come closer to the quantum future we all envision.

Finally, I thank TU Delft and edX for their comprehensive courses on quantum computing and quantum computer
technology. They helped me grasp these fascinating and complex subjects, and the hardware part of this site
draws heavily on them. I recommend them to anyone interested in the field.

I hope you enjoy exploring this website. Feel free to browse, learn and get in touch with any questions or
comments as you explore the different sides of my life and work. Whether you are drawn to my paintings or to
the technical work, I warmly invite you to come along on this journey; I am always glad to meet others who
share a passion for learning, innovation and positive impact.
